The workshop smelled of oil and sawdust before Mara even turned the key.

She liked that smell. It meant the benches were still hers, the gears still waiting, the day still unmade. Outside, the canal carried a thin morning mist. A barge horn sounded once, far off, and then the city settled back into its usual muttering.

Mara set her satchel on the stool by the door and rolled up her sleeves.

The first job was a pocket watch with a cracked crystal and a stem that stuck halfway out. The owner, a baker from the next street over, had left a note asking for mercy and speed. Mara wiped the case with a soft cloth, opened the back plate, and listened. The tick was uneven, like a runner trying to find a pace after a stumble.

She worked in silence for a while, the kind of silence that had weight to it. Tools clicked. A loupe caught the window light. She found grit in the winding arbor, cleaned it, oiled sparingly, and tested the mainspring tension with her thumb. Better. Not perfect, but honest.

By the time the baker returned, the watch kept time within a few seconds an hour. He paid without haggling and left a cinnamon roll on the counter as interest. Mara ate half of it standing up and saved the rest for later.

Eli appeared at noon, as he often did, with two cups and a question.

"Did you sleep?" he asked.

"Enough," Mara said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting before I finish the wall clock."

Eli leaned against the doorframe and watched her adjust the pendulum on a tall case piece a retired teacher had brought in last week. The clock had arrived with a story about a house that always ran ten minutes fast, as if time itself were eager to leave.

"You could delegate," Eli said.

"To who? The ghost in the attic?"

"To me."

Mara paused, hammer in hand. "You don't know escapements."

"I know how to hold a lamp steady."

She considered that, then pointed with her chin toward the bench. "Don't touch the blue tray. Everything else, ask first."

They fell into a rhythm. Eli held light. Mara worked. The afternoon stretched and narrowed and stretched again. Customers came and went: a girl with a music box that played only every third note, a man with cufflinks that hid broken clasps, an elderly couple who wanted their anniversary clock cleaned for a party they were throwing on Saturday.

Mara took each problem apart the way she took apart sentences when she read at night—carefully, with respect for what had been built before her.

The music box girl returned an hour later with her mother, who wanted to know if the missing notes could be coaxed back without replacing the cylinder. Mara showed them both how dust could mute a pin, how a single bent tooth could silence a song. The girl listened with the solemn attention children give to adults who treat them as capable. Mara liked that about her work: it made room for seriousness in small hands.

The cufflink man lingered too, not because he cared about clasps, but because he liked the smell of the shop and the excuse to stand out of the rain. He told Mara about a ferry he had taken as a boy, how the deck had vibrated with the engine and how his father had pointed at the horizon as if distance were a promise. Mara nodded at the right moments and filed the story away with all the others people handed her like loose change.

The anniversary couple brought sandwiches and stayed until Mara had to remind them, gently, that love did not require witnessing every turn of a screwdriver. They laughed and left the sandwiches anyway. Eli ate one later and declared it the best apology food he had ever tasted.

At two o'clock, Mara carried a tray of parts to the back room and realized she had not opened the back window all day. She pushed it up. Cool air slid in, carrying river damp and the faint sweetness of bread from the bakery line. For a moment the workshop felt less like a box and more like a room in the world.

She thought about trains then—not seriously, not yet—but enough to picture a window that moved while you slept.

At three o'clock, rain started against the windows.

The sound made the workshop feel smaller and safer. Mara brewed tea on the hot plate she wasn't supposed to keep plugged in. Eli read aloud from his notebook, not poetry, just observations: the color of the rain, the way the streetlights would come on early, the number of umbrellas passing the canal bridge.

"You're writing weather reports now?" Mara asked.

"I'm writing what I notice."

"Not much plot."

"Plot is for people with free time."

Mara snorted. Tea burned her tongue. She deserved it.

A delivery boy stumbled in with a crate of brass gears and an invoice he couldn't explain. Mara signed for it, sorted the sizes, and discovered an extra packet tucked under the straw—replacement crystals, unlisted, probably meant for another shop on another street. She called the supplier and spent ten minutes on hold listening to music that sounded like a clock dying in slow motion.

When she hung up, Eli had made a second pot of tea without being asked. They drank it too hot and complained together, which was almost companionship and almost enough.

The wall clock went out the door at four, ticking steady. The teacher hugged her, which was awkward and sincere. Mara wiped her hands on her apron and realized she hadn't eaten since the half cinnamon roll.

Between customers, Mara sketched a design for a mantel clock she would never build unless someone commissioned it: waves along the base, a moon phase disk she didn't know how to price. Eli looked over her shoulder and said the moon looked guilty. Mara said the moon always looked guilty when it was full.

"You should put that in the window," he said.

"And scare away serious clients?"

"You don't have serious clients. You have sentimental ones with broken heirlooms."

He wasn't wrong. Mara slid the sketch under a tray of screws and told herself she would revisit it on a slow day.

Slow days were a myth in this neighborhood.

Eli produced a paper bag from under his coat. "Rice balls. Don't ask where from."

"I wasn't going to."

They ate by the window, watching the rain thin into mist. The canal turned silver. A heron landed on a post and judged them both.

"You ever think about leaving?" Eli asked.

"Leaving where?"

"The city. The shop. The whole careful life."

Mara looked at her hands. They were nicked and stained and steady. "I think about it the way you think about jumping into cold water. Interesting idea. Bad timing."

"So never?"

"So not today."

He nodded as if she'd given him a full speech. Maybe she had.

Evening brought a different crowd: people who had put off repairs until they couldn't anymore. A barber with scissors that wouldn't close. A courier with a compass that pointed stubbornly east. A teenager with headphones around her neck and a wristwatch full of glitter glue.

Mara fixed what she could. She explained what she couldn't. She quoted fair prices and stuck to them.

The barber's scissors had rust in the hinge. Mara soaked them, dried them, aligned the blades, and demonstrated the snap on a strip of paper. The barber watched like a man seeing magic he refused to admire out loud. When he paid, he asked if she ever cut hair. Mara said she only cut time.

The courier's compass needed demagnetizing and a lecture about storing tools beside speaker wire. The teenager's glitter watch required patience, tweezers, and one swear word Mara swallowed before it reached her teeth. The girl blushed and said she could fix it herself next time. Mara believed her.

At eight, Eli left for a reading at the library. Mara walked him to the corner because the streetlamp there had been out for a week and she didn't like the dark stretch by the cooper's yard.

"Come by tomorrow," he said.

"I live here."

"You know what I mean."

She did. She watched him go, notebook tucked under his arm, shoulders hunched against a wind that hadn't arrived yet.

Back in the shop, Mara turned the sign to closed and swept. Sawdust curled into piles like pale hair. She oiled her own tools, replaced a worn file, wrote tomorrow's list in block letters on the back of a receipt.

The last item said: *ask Eli about the attic*.

She stared at that for a long time, then added a question mark and circled it twice.

Night settled over the canal. The barge horns grew rare. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Mara locked the front door, checked it twice, and climbed the narrow stairs to the apartment above the workshop.

Her rooms were small and orderly in the way of someone who spent all day managing chaos below. Books on shelves. A kettle on the stove. A chair with a blanket folded over the back, waiting.

She made cocoa she didn't need and sat by the window with the pocket watch from the morning still on the table. It ticked beside her cup, patient and smug.

Below, the canal reflected fragmented lights. A late barge pushed through the dark, its wake folding the reflections into brief disorder. Mara wondered how many people on that barge were carrying something broken, something they hoped someone else could make whole.

She had wanted to be that someone since she was small enough to stand on a stool and watch her grandmother replace a watch crystal with hands that never shook. Her grandmother had not smiled much, but she had been precise, and precision had felt like love without performance.

Mara still worked that way. It was not warmth, exactly. It was reliability, which some people mistook for warmth and others mistook for distance.

Eli mistook it for challenge. That was why he kept coming back.

Tomorrow there would be more broken things. More questions. More rain, probably.

Tonight she listened to the tick and let her mind wander down alleys it usually avoided: trains, coastlines, rooms with no clocks at all.

The watch kept time. Mara, for once, did not.

She slept a little before midnight, woke to the same tick, and smiled at the ceiling.

Some problems could be solved. Others only had to be carried.

In the morning, the baker came back—not for the watch, which still ran, but for a second opinion on a kitchen timer that screamed when it reached zero. Mara took it apart and found a loose contact laughing at everyone.

"Fixed," she said.

"You're a miracle worker," the baker said.

"I'm a person with small screwdrivers."

He laughed and left another roll. Mara decided the universe was trying to feed her into a better mood.

Eli arrived late, with ink on his fingers and an apology already forming.

"Library ran long," he said.

"You say that every week."

"Because it's true every week."

They worked side by side until the sun crossed the high windows. Mara taught him how to brace a mainspring without swearing. He taught her a word she'd never heard for the color of old brass. The day passed the way long days do when you stop fighting them.

At lunch they walked to the market square because Mara had promised herself fresh apples and because Eli liked watching people invent reasons to argue about produce. A fishmonger and a flute seller were in a heated debate about noise pollution. Mara bought four apples and one pear she did not need. Eli bought nothing and wrote three pages.

In the afternoon, an old man brought a ship's chronometer in a wooden box lined with velvet that had gone bald in patches. He said it had belonged to his brother, who had gone to sea and not come back. Mara cleaned the movement without speaking much. Some stories did not need commentary. They only needed time and clean oil and someone willing to listen to the tick without demanding a moral.

When the old man left, Eli asked if that kind of job ever got heavy.

"Everything heavy gets lighter if you do it often enough," Mara said.

"That's either wisdom or a warning."

"Can't it be both?"

By closing, the shop was cleaner than it had been in months. Mara wrote that down too, on the same receipt list, under *attic?*.

She meant to ask him at the corner. She meant to say yes to something unnamed.

Instead, the streetlamp flickered on—new bulb, city crew—and Mara stood under it longer than she needed to, feeling the day settle into memory.

The long draft of her life kept ticking forward, one honest second at a time.
